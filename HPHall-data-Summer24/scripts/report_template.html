Technical report · Hall-thruster latent dynamics

Embedding the Breathing Mode

A convolutional autoencoder compresses a Hall-thruster plasma simulation into a handful of numbers; a small reservoir computer learns to run their dynamics on its own. Three latent numbers are enough to reproduce the discharge's breathing-mode limit cycle. Two are not — and the reason is topological, not statistical.

adrianskw / AE‑RC HPHall-data-Summer24 SPT-100-class thruster, 300 V / 5.01 mg·s-1 Xe

Summary

A 7-field, 50×25 plasma fluid simulation (20,000 frames, 1 ms of a breathing-mode oscillation) is compressed by a convolutional autoencoder into a 3-number latent trajectory. A 200-node reservoir computer, driven only by that 3-number series, learns to reproduce the oscillation on its own: run with no external input at all, it free-runs the correct limit-cycle shape at the correct frequency to within 3.4%. Repeating the entire pipeline with a 2-number latent space instead gives an autoencoder that is almost as accurate at static reconstruction — but whose latent trajectory self-intersects, and whose reservoir cannot sustain an autonomous oscillation at all: it collapses to a fixed point. Three latent variables are not an arbitrary round number here; they appear to be the minimum needed to embed this attractor without ambiguity.

1. Background & pipeline

The underlying data is an HPHall-II simulation of an SPT-100-class Hall-effect thruster (300 V discharge, 5.01 mg/s xenon, 0.02 T radial field), saved every 50 ns for 20,000 iterations (1.00 ms simulated). Each frame holds 7 fields — electron density ne, potential φ, electron temperature Te, neutral density nn, ionization rate ṅi, and axial/radial ion velocity vi,z, vi,r — on a 50×25 grid, and the whole run is dominated by the classic breathing-mode instability: a global, quasi-periodic oscillation of the discharge.

The pipeline has three stages, and the autoencoder and reservoir are trained and evaluated completely separately:

field grid (7×25×50) ↓ [ frozen conv-autoencoder encoder ] latent vector z ∈ R^D (D = 3, or D = 2 for comparison) ↓ [ reservoir computer, N = 200 nodes ] predicted latent ẑ (driven, or fully autonomous) ↓ [ same frozen autoencoder, decoder half ] predicted field grid (7×25×50)

The autoencoder is a small 3-layer convolutional encoder/decoder (89,034 trainable parameters for D = 3; 77,385 for D = 2 — the difference is entirely in the two linear layers either side of the bottleneck). It was trained in two passes: 200 epochs on a 2,000-frame subsample, then warm-started and fine-tuned for 60 more epochs on the complete 20,000-frame run. All splits are chronological (85% / 15%, i.e. the last 3,000 frames are held out) rather than random, so "generalizes" means "extrapolates to unseen future cycles," not just interpolates.

The reservoir is a mapRC from libRC: 200 nodes, spectral radius 0.9, connection degree 10 (≈5% density), driven by the AE's latent series and fit with ridge regression (α = 10-3). Only the linear readout is trained — the reservoir's internal connectivity is fixed random weights, so the entire learned system is a D×201 matrix (603 numbers for D = 3). Three regimes are tested on the held-out window: listen/train (fit quality while driven by the true signal), echo (disconnect entirely and run autonomously — the real test of whether the attractor's dynamics were learned), and infer (drive with only z1 and estimate the rest, a partial-observability test).

Does more reservoir capacity help? N=200 vs. N=1,000

The 200-node reservoir above is deliberately small. Repeating the identical fit with a 1,000-node reservoir (same ρ, degree, and ridge α) keeps the same 3-dimensional embedding but gives the readout far more room to represent the map from reservoir state to next latent step.

Capacity helps the well-posed case

At N=1,000 the autonomous period mismatch drops to __PERIOD_MISMATCH3_N1000__% (from __PERIOD_MISMATCH3__% at N=200), mean echo RMSE in latent space falls from __ECHO_RMSE3_N200__ to __ECHO_RMSE3_N1000__, and the driven infer PC improves slightly to __PC_Z2_3_N1000__ / __PC_Z3_3_N1000__ (from 0.978 / 0.946). Decoded through the same frozen AE, echo error stops climbing monotonically and instead oscillates within a bounded range as the reservoir settles onto a stable, correctly-shaped limit cycle.

For reference: repeating this same N=200→1,000 jump on the 2-latent embedding does not reproduce this improvement. Its autonomous mode stops collapsing to a fixed point, but rather than finding the true limit cycle it settles onto a smaller, offset, spurious one that avoids the self-intersection — echo RMSE actually gets slightly worse (0.98→1.17). More capacity fixes an under-fit reservoir; it cannot fix a latent space that is not a valid embedding of the attractor in the first place — consistent with §5 below.

How much does the reservoir's own architecture matter?

Every result above uses one fixed connectivity (degree 10, i.e. each node wired to 10 others) and one fixed update rule (mapRC's discrete map). Two further sweeps, still entirely in latent space (no decoding), probe how much those choices matter: (1) the connectivity degree of the mapRC reservoir, swept 0–10 at N=200; and (2) for a diffRC reservoir — the same node but updated by integrating an ODE (ṙ = −r + tanh(Ar+By), via RK2) rather than iterating a discrete map — both degree (0–10) and the integration step ds (0.001–1), a 77-point grid.

Sparser wiring is better for mapRC's free run

Autonomous echo is sharply better with almost no internal connectivity: mean echo RMSE is __MAP_ECHO_RMSE_LOW__ at degree 0–1, versus __MAP_ECHO_RMSE_HIGH__ across degree 2–10 — roughly 2–3× worse, with no further trend as degree keeps climbing. Driven infer, by contrast, barely moves: PC for the unobserved channels stays in the 0.92–0.98 band at every degree, peaking at degree __MAP_BEST_PC_DEGREE__ (__MAP_BEST_PC_Z2__ / __MAP_BEST_PC_Z3__). Extra random recurrent connections add dynamical complexity the ridge readout never has to correct for while being driven, but that same complexity destabilizes the free-running attractor.

diffRC is far less forgiving than mapRC

Where every mapRC degree gave a usable reservoir, __DIFF_N_FAIL__ of __DIFF_N_TOTAL__ diffRC (ds, degree) combinations produced a driven infer that is essentially uncorrelated with the truth (PC < 0.1) despite a perfectly reasonable-looking echo RMSE — the readout fits the training data but the ODE-integrated state trajectory it relies on doesn't reproduce under re-driving. This isn't confined to one regime: degree=0 fails this way at every ds ≥ 0.1, and mid-size ds (0.003–0.03) fail this way at nearly every degree ≥ 1 — a resonance between the fixed ridge gain and the RK2 step size that mapRC, having no step size to begin with, simply cannot exhibit.

...but the best diffRC point edges out mapRC's best

The single best combination found, ds=__DIFF_BEST_DS__, degree=__DIFF_BEST_DEGREE__, reaches mean echo RMSE __DIFF_BEST_ECHO_RMSE__ with infer PC __DIFF_BEST_PC_Z2__ / __DIFF_BEST_PC_Z3__ — simultaneously lower echo error and comparable-or-better driven reconstruction than mapRC's own best degree-0 echo (__MAP_ECHO_RMSE_LOW__) or best-PC configuration (__MAP_BEST_PC_Z2__ / __MAP_BEST_PC_Z3__ at degree __MAP_BEST_PC_DEGREE__). The catch is that this point sits in a narrow, non-obvious pocket of the grid rather than on any clear trend line: neighboring ds values a factor of 3–10× away land back in the failure regime above. mapRC's degree sweep is a smooth, forgiving trade-off; diffRC trades that robustness for a slightly better ceiling if ds is tuned specifically for this system.

Does the autoencoder's capacity matter to the RC?

Every result above uses the "production" AE (16/32/64 channels, warm-started on a 2,000-frame subsample before fine-tuning on the full set). The RC itself is a deliberately small, lightweight model — does the AE feeding it need to be as large as it is? Three architecturally-scaled variants, each trained from scratch for 60 epochs on the full 20,000-frame set with no warm start, isolate parameter count as the only difference: light (8/16/32 channels, __AE_LIGHT_PARAMS__ params), base (16/32/64, __AE_BASE_PARAMS__ params — same architecture as production), and heavy (32/64/128, __AE_HEAVY_PARAMS__ params).

Capacity buys diminishing reconstruction returns

Mean per-field reconstruction error drops from __AE_LIGHT_ERR__% (light) to __AE_BASE_ERR__% (base) to __AE_HEAVY_ERR__% (heavy) — most of the gain comes from the first step up, not the second. The ionization-rate field ṅi is by far the most size-sensitive: __AE_LIGHT_NIDOT__% → __AE_BASE_NIDOT__% → __AE_HEAVY_NIDOT__%, roughly double every other field's error at every size. Going lighter costs real fidelity; going heavier than "base" buys comparatively little.

Better AE, better echo, worse infer — and diffRC breaks outright

Echo RMSE improves monotonically with AE size for both RC types, as expected from a cleaner latent signal. Driven infer PC does the opposite: it degrades as the AE gets bigger, and diffRC on the heavy AE's latent collapses outright (PC≈0.19/0.01, essentially uncorrelated with truth) despite an unremarkable echo RMSE. Part of this is a real effect and part is a fitting artifact worth flagging: these "best" hyperparameters were tuned specifically against the production AE's particular trained instance, not against AE capacity in general — even base, which matches production's architecture exactly, reaches only __RCSIZE_BASE_MAP_PC__ infer PC here versus __PC_Z2_3__ / __PC_Z3_3__ for the actual production AE earlier in this report. The RC's sensitivity is not just to how big the AE is, but to the specific local minimum the AE training happened to land in.

3. Results: two latent variables

Repeating the identical pipeline with a 2-number latent space (77,385 AE parameters) gives a fine-tuned validation MSE of __FINAL_VAL2__ — only modestly worse than the 3-latent __FINAL_VAL3__. Static per-field errors move by well under a percentage point across the board. By reconstruction accuracy alone, two numbers look almost sufficient.

Key finding

The 2-latent reservoir's listen/train fit error (__FIT_ERR2__) is comparable to the 3-latent case, and its driven infer mode still works reasonably (PC = __PC_Z2_2__ for the unobserved channel, down from 0.978/0.946). But its echo mode fails qualitatively, not just quantitatively: instead of drifting out of phase like the 3-latent case, it never sustains a cycle at all. Collapsing to the mean latent state acts like predicting the time-averaged field — a statistically sensible fallback that keeps the raw error from looking catastrophic, but it is not the dynamics of a breathing-mode thruster.

5. Why three? An embedding-dimension argument

The static-reconstruction numbers alone would suggest two latent variables are nearly as good as three. The dynamics say otherwise, and the reason is topological rather than statistical.

A simple closed loop — a single-frequency limit cycle — can always be drawn in a plane without crossing itself; that is just what a circle is. If the breathing mode were that simple, a 2-latent embedding should have produced a clean loop, the same way the 3-latent one did. It didn't: Fig. 6 shows a genuine self-intersection, meaning two different physical states of the plasma were mapped to the same (z₁, z₂) point. That is not a training failure of the autoencoder — it is a direct, empirical demonstration that the true attractor is not a simple 1-D circle. It has enough additional structure (weak higher-harmonic content, or a genuinely quasi-periodic rather than strictly periodic character) that no 2-dimensional projection can represent it without ambiguity.

This is the same obstruction that motivates Takens' delay-embedding theorem in dynamical systems: reconstructing an attractor of intrinsic (box-counting) dimension d from observations generically requires an embedding space of at least 2d + 1 dimensions to guarantee no spurious self-intersections; fewer dimensions fold the attractor over itself. Takens' theorem is stated for delay-coordinate embeddings of a scalar time series, not for a learned autoencoder bottleneck, but the topological argument transfers directly: an embedding is an embedding, however it is constructed, and a 2-dimensional target space cannot faithfully represent an attractor whose effective dimension exceeds 1.

The reservoir results make the consequence concrete rather than abstract. A learned autonomous map on an ambiguous, self-crossing state space has no consistent vector field to follow at the crossing point — two different "next states" are equally valid continuations of the same current state. The 2-latent reservoir's response was to settle onto a fixed point that avoids the contradiction altogether, rather than to sustain a cycle that would eventually have to pass back through the ambiguous region. The 3-latent reservoir, given a genuinely unambiguous embedding, had no such obstruction — and its only remaining error (a 3.4% period mismatch) is an ordinary least-squares fitting imprecision, not a structural one.

6. Conclusion

Three latent variables reproduce the Hall-thruster breathing mode's limit cycle almost exactly under free-running (autonomous) reservoir dynamics, with the only meaningful residual error being a small, ordinary period mismatch that compounds over many cycles. Two latent variables reconstruct individual frames almost as well, but cannot support autonomous dynamics at all: the latent space itself is not a valid embedding of the attractor, and the reservoir's only stable option is to give up on oscillating. The practical takeaway for this kind of latent-dynamics pipeline: validate the latent embedding's topology, not just its reconstruction error, before asking a downstream dynamical model to learn on top of it — a bottleneck can look fine by MSE and still be the wrong shape to build dynamics on.
